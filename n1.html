Supply structure · the N−1 test

Can the rest cope without the leader?

The sharpest test of a chokepoint is the N−1 question: if the dominant supplier stopped, how much of its own demand could everyone else combined actually meet? By 2035, the answer ranges from 82% for lithium — nearly self-sufficient — to just 28% for graphite, where the rest of the world cannot come close. The gap tells you which chokepoints a stockpile or friend-shoring can close, and which need capacity built from scratch.

A high market share is alarming; an N−1 gap is actionable. It nets out demand and supply outside the dominant supplier and asks whether the remainder can stand alone. Where the bar is near full (lithium), a disruption is survivable with inventory. Where it is short (graphite, magnets), no stockpile is large enough — the only fix is years of new midstream capacity, which is exactly the built-capability chokepoint the map flags.

Ex-dominant-supplier coverage of demand, 2035

How much of the dominant supplier's own demand the rest of the world's projected capacity could cover if that supplier were removed. Higher = more resilient.

resilient (≥60%) exposed (30–60%) critical (<30%)▏ marker = full self-sufficiency (100%)
What the gaps tell you to do. Lithium (82%) and nuclear enrichment (~90% ex-top-2) are close to self-healing — a modest strategic reserve bridges any shock. Nickel (36%) and rare-earth refining (~25%) are exposed — friend-shoring helps but capacity must expand. Graphite (28%) and rare-earth magnets (<20%) are critical — no realistic stockpile covers the gap, so the answer is sustained investment in midstream and downstream capacity, on a multi-year timeline. The N−1 number turns "who dominates" into "and can we live without them."

Source: IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 — N−1 analysis (Ch. 2, pp. 121-122, 226) and the diversification-pipeline figures for rare earths (mining ~50% / refining ~25% / magnets <20% of ex-China demand by 2035). © IEA, CC BY 4.0.
