Decision layer · the buffer

The stockpile that mostly isn’t there

Half the atlas’s remedies end in the same word: stockpile. When a chokepoint can’t be out-built or substituted quickly, a strategic reserve is the bridge. So how much of a public buffer does the West actually hold? For most critical materials, the honest answer is none — and one number frames the US case: its strategic stockpile holds about 6% of its own identified dollar shortfall (a budget gap, not days of cover).

An honest, data-thin page. Strategic-reserve holdings are the least transparent numbers in this field — China’s are secret, and even Western figures are patchy. So this does not compute a precise days-of-cover for all 32; it reports the public buffer, which is the gap. Figures web-verified from CRS, GAO, Columbia CGEP and JOGMEC; reproducible: build_stockpiles.py → stockpiles.json.

Why this is the quiet decision

Every other lever the atlas measures — build an alternative supplier, substitute the material, recycle it — takes years. A stockpile is the only one that works on the timescale of an actual shock: it buys the months or years the others need. That is exactly why its absence matters. For the geological chokepoints (phosphate, the platinum-group metals, niobium) there is no faster option — you cannot out-build the rock — so the stockpile is not a backstop but the front line, and for most of them the West holds nothing. Japan drew the right lesson from its 2010 rare-earth shock and built a 60-to-180-day buffer; the US let a Cold-War stockpile wither to ~6% of its own identified dollar shortfall; the EU has a plan and no reserve; and China, which holds the chokepoints, also holds an undisclosed reserve behind them. One honest qualifier: government reserves aren’t the only cushion — commercial inventories, scrap mobilisation and rationing all absorb part of a shock — but those are private and unplanned, and for the geological chokepoints there is no substitute for a deliberate public buffer.
