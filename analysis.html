These pages test one question from every angle: where the concentration in critical-material supply chains really sits, and what to do about it. Here is what they collectively show — then the full set, organised in four groups, below.

What these analyses collectively show

Four conclusions the pages below reach from different angles. Follow a thread, or browse the full set underneath.

1

The chokepoint is the refiner, not the mine

Control sits at the furnace: for 17 of 32 materials the top exporter is a processor, not a miner — and the gap survives in tonnes and isn’t transshipment.

the refining wedge → origin trace → why RCA misses it

2

By-product metals are structurally trapped

Metals pulled from a host ore can’t be out-mined — their output is bounded by how much host is refined, not by their own price — and they recycle at half the rate of primary metals. A dependency no new mine answers. (Supply inelasticity, not price-coupling — the price claim was tested and withdrawn.)

hostage metals → the host-facility map → no way out

3

The concentration is real, and survives scrutiny

Measured in tonnes not dollars it holds for 28 of 32 materials; weighted by governance the ranking sharpens rather than dissolves.

value vs volume → tonnes + governance → the governance flip

4

Not every dependency is fixed the same way

Some a stockpile can bridge, some need years of capacity; some chokepoints are easing, some entrenching — and only 3 of 32 are a mine you can’t rebuild.

N−1 resilience → easing vs entrenching → the decision layer
