What you can trust

Built to be broken — here is what survives.

Most dashboards ask you to trust them. This one shows its work and tries to break itself. Every number traces to an open script; ten automated guards run on every change; and the atlas has been through three rounds of adversarial red-team review that caught — and published — three real errors. This page is the short version: what the atlas can say with confidence, and how it knows.

The discipline

Rigor is not a claim; it is a set of habits you can check. These are the atlas’s.

The rule throughout: never hand-tune a number to look right. Where the data cannot settle a question, the atlas reports the range and says so — concentration, for instance, is shown as a spread across four reconstructions, not a single figure, because no reconstruction of customs data is ground truth.

What the atlas can say with confidence

The findings below rest on robust rows only — and each ends where a finding should: with what to do about it.

The chokepoint is rarely the mine

Across 58 end-use supply chains, only 5 are constrained by geology — a deposit that cannot be moved. The other 53 concentrate at a stage that was built: a refinery, a fab, a separation plant, an export rule.

So what: most dependencies are not permanent. An export ban on ore cannot touch a refining chokepoint — but capital, energy policy and years of qualification can rebuild one. Target the stage that concentrates, not the mine. The Chokepoint Map →

The seller is usually not the source

For 17 of 32 materials the top exporter never mined the material — it refined someone else’s ore and re-sold it. The gap holds when measured in tonnes, not just dollars, and survives a permutation test (it is far more than chance).

So what: a customs ledger of “who exports” systematically mislocates the real dependency. Trace to the refiner, and to the mine behind it, before you name a supplier secure. The origin gap →

Concentration is moving — and we can date it

On the robust series, export concentration is rising for coking coal, manganese, bauxite, rare-earth magnets, magnesium, tungsten and strontium — several already single-country dominated (magnets, magnesium and tungsten toward China; bauxite toward Guinea). It is falling for copper, nickel, palladium, helium, vanadium and tantalum.

So what: the materials tightening are not always the ones in the headlines. A rising-and-already-dominated material (magnesium, tungsten) is where a shock would bite hardest and where diversification has the most to undo. The tested trends →

When the chokepoint is the mine, geology says why

The five geological chokepoints — phosphate rock, the platinum-group metals, boron, strontium, and niobium (the steel-alloy chain) — cannot be out-built. There is no second Bushveld for the platinum-group metals, no second Araxá for niobium.

So what: for these, the toolkit is not “build capacity elsewhere.” It is substitution, recycling and stockpiling — a different, slower policy, and worth starting early. Why the mechanism matters →

What we got wrong (on purpose visible)

A research site that never corrects itself is not being checked. The atlas keeps a public ledger of its own errors — because the corrections are the evidence the method works.

  • A price-coupling claim, withdrawn. We had argued by-product metals track their host’s price; a proper test on real price series refuted the general law (only bismuth←lead survived). The supply claim stands; the price claim was retracted.
  • Cobalt, diagnosed backwards, then corrected. A first pass called a cobalt trend conflict a “thin-code artifact” on a buggy extraction. Screening thinness across every commodity up front exposed the error: cobalt is a real $0.8–4.9B market, and the finding is that the engine understated DR Congo’s real dominance — a genuine limitation, kept in view.
  • A screen that flagged the wrong thing. An early fragility rule mislabelled nine genuinely-concentrated commodities (bauxite, magnets, tungsten) as unreliable. Fixed: single-country dominance is a finding, not a data flaw. The real fragile list is three.
