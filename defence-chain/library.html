Research Library · Defence

Defence / military-materials chain

A curated reference library of public sources for deeper study — gathered August 2026. Each entry links to a real source and carries an abstract (or factual summary) plus the data it rests on. A research-support layer of the Critical Materials Atlas.

Radar, night vision, precision-guided munitions and the shells themselves run on a short list of materials — gallium, germanium, antimony, tungsten, rare-earth magnets — whose mining and refining is concentrated in one country, which has already export-controlled or banned them to the United States. A defence "critical minerals" list is not a supply-risk table — it is a map of a rival's leverage.

Ordered to follow the chain: ① Materials (the China-held upstream) → ② components and the weapon-system capability each enables → ③ the leverage, already demonstrated through export controls → ④ the Western response (stockpiles and the industrial base) — then the cross-cutting criticality frameworks and trade data that sit across every stage.

① The materials — the China-held upstream 3

Not the biggest materials by tonnage — the ones a modern weapon cannot be built without and cannot quickly re-source.
  • USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 — Gallium · USGS (2025) Annual world production/reserves/trade data for gallium; the source of the ~98–99% refined-China share. Summary. USGS commodity summary: China supplied about 98% of the world's primary refined gallium and 99% of low-purity gallium; world high-purity refined output was ~320,000 kg in 2024. US net import reliance is 100%. Integrated circuits took 79% of US consumption, optoelectronics 20%; 2023 Chinese export controls spurred efforts to restart domestic output. Data. USGS annual producer canvass plus reported trade data; production largely a by-product of aluminium (bauxite) and zinc refining.
  • USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 — Germanium · USGS (2025) Annual germanium supply/use data underlying the IR-optics and night-vision material link. Summary. USGS commodity summary: China is the leading germanium producer and banned all germanium exports to the US in December 2024. US germanium-metal imports (2020–23) were 51% China, 27% Belgium, 15% Germany. Major US end uses are fibre optics, infrared optics (lenses for thermal/night-vision systems), semiconductors and solar cells; a 2025 DoD award funded domestic wafer capacity. Data. USGS annual producer canvass plus reported trade data; germanium recovered as a by-product of zinc ores and coal fly ash.
  • USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 — Antimony · USGS (2025) Annual antimony mine-production and trade data; feeds the antimony material-share bar. Summary. USGS commodity summary: China is the world's largest antimony producer (~40,000 t of ~110,000 t world mine output). US net import reliance is 85%, with an estimated 54% of consumption met by imports from China; antimony oxide imports are 76% China. Main uses are flame retardants, lead-acid batteries, and munitions (primers, tracers, hardened alloy). Data. USGS annual producer canvass plus reported import/export trade data; no primary US mine production.

② Components → weapon-system capability 4

Where a material becomes a component becomes a capability — and where the public bill of materials runs out, so the mapping turns qualitative.
  • China's New Rare Earth and Magnet Restrictions Threaten U.S. Defense Supply Chains · CSIS Defense-specific framing of the rare-earth / magnet restrictions, with per-platform material content. Summary. CSIS analysis: China's controls threaten US platforms — an F-35 needs over 900 lb of rare earths, a Virginia-/Columbia-class submarine ~9,200 lb, plus Tomahawks, radars, drones and smart bombs. A foreign-direct-product rule blocks magnets with even 0.1% Chinese content; China holds ~70% of mining, ~90% of processing, ~93% of magnet manufacture. Data. Analysis of Chinese export-control notices plus published per-platform rare-earth content and processing-share estimates; no primary dataset.
  • Minerals, Magnets, and Military Capability: China's Rare Earth Weaponization Should Be a Wake-Up Call · Modern War Institute (West Point) Military-academy analysis framing rare-earth export control as demonstrated weaponization. Summary. Argues China's rare-earth controls are strategic weaponisation threatening US readiness: China refines over 85% of the world's rare earths and makes nearly 90% of high-performance magnets. Proposes restocking the National Defense Stockpile with finished magnets, accelerating domestic production, and binding allied partnerships. Notes GAO's $18.5B remediation estimate and a sub-$1B stockpile (vs ~$42B at Cold-War peak). Data. Policy analysis citing processing-share estimates, GAO cost figures and stockpile valuations; no primary dataset.
  • Geopolitical risk and the global supply of rare earth permanent magnets: Insights from China's export trends · Energy Economics (2025) Quantitative analysis of geopolitical risk in the rare-earth magnet supply chain via Chinese export data. Abstract. Using multiple linear regression on Chinese national export figures for 2017–2024, the study finds geopolitical risk exerts a markedly positive effect on the supply of rare-earth permanent magnets: a 1% rise in geopolitical risk correlates with a ~3.8-tonne increase in magnet exports — suggesting China uses high-volume exports to reinforce market dominance rather than hoarding to punish rivals. (Full text behind a publisher wall — abstract taken from the record and public indexing; authors Depraiter, Goutte & Porcher.) Data. Chinese national rare-earth-magnet export figures 2017–2024 against a geopolitical-risk index; multiple linear regression.
  • Securing defense critical minerals: Challenges and U.S. strategic responses in an evolving geopolitical landscape · Comparative Strategy (2025) Peer-reviewed review of US strategic responses to defense-critical-mineral dependency. Abstract. Peer-reviewed review (V. Vivoda, Comparative Strategy 44:2) of the growing dependence on critical minerals for advanced military technologies. Identifies key defence-critical minerals, emphasises their dual-use nature and the risk of reliance on adversarial states such as China and Russia, analyses US strategic responses, and recommends balancing national security, economic feasibility and sustainability. (Full article behind a publisher wall — summarised from the abstract and public indexing.) Data. Qualitative review / policy synthesis of US defence-critical-mineral strategy; no primary dataset.

③ The leverage — export controls demonstrated 8

The upstream position is not theoretical: 2023–2024 turned it into a used lever, with an observable price and volume response.
  • China Imposes Its Most Stringent Critical Minerals Export Restrictions Yet · CSIS (2024) Analysis of the December 2024 gallium/germanium/antimony export ban to the US. Summary. CSIS analysis: in December 2024 China banned exports of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the US — its first country-specific ban — retaliating for new US semiconductor-equipment controls on 140 Chinese firms. China holds 48% of global antimony output and 63% of US antimony imports; a full Ga/Ge ban could cost ~$3.4B in US GDP. Data. Analysis of Chinese Ministry of Commerce notices plus USGS production/import-reliance and GDP-loss estimates.
  • China export-control measures for antimony and related items · Global Trade Alert (2024) Primary policy-tracking record of the August 2024 antimony export controls. Summary. Policy-tracking record: China's Ministry of Commerce announced export-licensing requirements for antimony and related items in August 2024, covering 17 HS codes and effective 15 September 2024, with no revocation date. Global Trade Alert classifies the measure as "certainly harmful". Data. Structured policy-intervention record from Global Trade Alert's state-act monitoring; no market dataset.
  • Uncertainty lingers over antimony exports following US-China truce · Fastmarkets (2025) Market reporting on antimony volume/price response and the trade truce. Summary. Market report: China's antimony export controls (from September 2024, tightened May 2025) stalled shipments, with containers held at ports for months and most exporters suspending overseas business. After the November Trump-Xi meeting, participants awaited clarity on whether US exports would resume. Data. Qualitative market commentary and trader interviews; no quantified volume/price series on this page.
  • Ministry of Commerce Notice 2024 No. 46 — controls on dual-use items to the United States · CSET, Georgetown (2024) Translation/analysis of China's formal notice tightening dual-use export controls to the US. Summary. CSET translation of China's Ministry of Commerce Notice 2024 No. 46 (3 December 2024): prohibits military-use exports and bans commercial shipments of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the United States, and tightens checks on dual-use graphite — issued a day after new US microchip export controls. Data. Primary-document translation of the Ministry of Commerce notice; analytical commentary, no dataset.
  • The Consequences of China's New Rare Earths Export Restrictions · CSIS Analysis of the April 2025 heavy-rare-earth and magnet export restrictions. Summary. CSIS analysis: in April 2025 China imposed licensing on seven medium/heavy rare earths — samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, yttrium — and magnets, threatening export pauses that especially hit listed US defence contractors. China held ~99% of heavy-REE processing until 2023. MP Materials' ~1,300 t/yr is under 1% of China's ~300,000 t of magnet output. Data. Analysis of Chinese licensing notices plus processing-share and production-capacity estimates; no primary dataset.
  • Rare Earth Export Restrictions One Year Later · CSIS One-year retrospective on China's rare-earth export restrictions and downstream effects. Summary. CSIS retrospective: a year after the April 2025 restrictions, China's yttrium exports to the US had collapsed from 333 t to 17 t. The US responded with ~$7.3B of government financing across mining, processing and magnet manufacturing (MP Materials, Vulcan Elements, others) and partnerships with Australia, Saudi Arabia, Japan and Malaysia. 2025 US REE imports were 71% China. Data. US production/import figures, export-flow series (yttrium tonnages) and itemised federal financing commitments across five agencies.
  • With new export controls on critical minerals, supply concentration risks become reality · IEA commentary IEA's read on how export controls turn theoretical supply-concentration risk into realised disruption. Summary. IEA commentary: China's October 2025 controls on rare earths and lithium-ion batteries turned concentration risk into live disruption across autos, defence and semiconductors. China refines ~91% of rare earths; its permanent-magnet share rose from ~50% two decades ago to 94%. 2024 magnet exports were 58,000 t; post-April-2025 European prices reached up to six times Chinese levels. Data. IEA supply-concentration analysis using refining/manufacturing shares, magnet export volumes and regional price differentials.
  • China's Mineral Export Ban Strikes at the US Defense Industrial Base · The Diplomat (2024) Regional-affairs framing of the December 2024 ban's direct defense-industrial impact. Summary. The Diplomat: on 3 December 2024 China's Ministry of Commerce barred exports of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the US. Antimony goes into bullets and artillery rounds, gallium into radar integrated circuits, germanium into night-vision and thermal sensors — so the immediate-effect ban risks near-term shortages, price rises and delayed munitions production. Data. Trade-press analysis of the export notice plus qualitative material-to-munition mapping; no primary dataset.

④ The Western response — stockpiles & the industrial base 10

The gap between the material list and the response is the whole risk: no domestic supply of key inputs, and a buffer built for a different era.
  • Emergency Access to Strategic and Critical Materials: The National Defense Stockpile (R47833) · Congressional Research Service (2023) Background on the legal authority and mechanics of the US National Defense Stockpile. Summary. CRS report (14 Nov 2023): the DoD-managed National Defense Stockpile gives domestic manufacturers emergency access to essential inputs "in the interest of national defense only". As of April 2023, inventory ($912.3M) covered only 37.9% of projected military shortfalls, 7.5% of civilian shortfalls and 6.2% of total net shortfalls; the FY2023 assessment found net shortfalls in 88 materials worth $14.83B. Data. Secondary synthesis of DoD stockpile-assessment figures, statute and materials-shortfall valuations; no new primary dataset.
  • National Defense Stockpile: Actions Needed to Improve DOD's Efforts to Prepare for Emergencies (GAO-24-106959) · U.S. GAO (2024) Audit finding gaps in DOD's stockpile preparedness for supply disruptions. Summary. GAO audit: DoD lacks data to model requirements for over 40% of its strategic and critical materials, and material shortfalls rose 167% between FY2019 and FY2023 (37 to 99 materials). Over 90% of shortfall materials had zero or one domestic supplier. GAO made six recommendations on data, processes and reporting, all concurred with. Data. GAO review of DoD stockpile requirement-modelling, shortfall lists and congressional reporting; supplier-concentration counts.
  • Critical Mineral Stockpiling Presents Opportunities · National Defense Magazine (2026) Coverage of the Pentagon's 2025 intent to procure up to $1B in stockpile materials. Summary. Government-contracting commentary on the DoD's 2025 intent to procure up to $1 billion in National Defense Stockpile materials — scandium, tungsten, graphite, samarium, dysprosium, terbium and related critical minerals — and the contracting opportunities that opens for domestic suppliers. (Page served as a script shell — summarised from the outlet's headline and matching public reporting.) Data. Trade-press commentary citing DoD/DLA procurement notices; no primary dataset.
  • Defense Logistics Agency Considers Dipping into Strategic Mineral Stockpiles · National Defense Magazine (2025) Reporting on DLA stockpile-drawdown deliberations amid supply pressure. Summary. Report: the Defense Logistics Agency, which holds ~48 minerals and alloys across six US sites as a "break-glass" reserve, is nearing the point of drawdown as China trade disputes cause shortages. A structural limit is noted — the military is often only ~1% of demand for many of these minerals, so a defence-only stockpile has limited market reach. (Page served as a script shell — summarised from public reporting of the article.) Data. Trade-press reporting citing DLA statements; no primary dataset.
  • Federal Push for Critical Minerals Stockpiling: 2025 in Review and Outlook for 2026 · Inside Government Contracts (2026) Year-in-review of US federal critical-minerals stockpiling policy. Summary. Year-in-review: in 2025 the DLA announced ~$1 billion of stockpile procurements (RFIs for scandium, tungsten, graphite and rare earths), and Congress appropriated $2 billion to the NDS Transaction Fund. Materials in focus span copper, nickel, lithium, samarium, dysprosium, terbium, bismuth, vanadium, indium and fluorspar; 2026 is expected to bring greater interagency coordination. Data. Policy synthesis of DoD/DLA notices and appropriations; no primary dataset.
  • Pentagon moves to build $1 billion critical minerals stockpile to counter China · MINING.com Trade-press report on the Pentagon's stockpiling drive and its itemised buys. Summary. Report (citing DLA filings): the Pentagon is acquiring up to $1 billion of critical minerals — including up to $500M of cobalt and $245M of antimony from US Antimony Corporation, $100M of tantalum, and ~$45M of scandium from Rio Tinto and APL. It sits within the ~$7.5B critical-minerals allocation of the 2025 budget act. (Direct page returned 403 this pass — itemised figures taken from matching public reporting of the same DLA filings.) Data. Trade-press report citing Defense Logistics Agency public procurement filings.
  • Minerals at War: Strategic Resources and the Foundations of the U.S. Defense Industrial Base · CSIS Broader framing of strategic minerals as the foundation of the US defense industrial base. Summary. CSIS report: mineral security is a permanent national-security challenge needing continuous stewardship, not episodic crisis response. It traces recurring cycles of wartime mobilisation and peacetime dismantling — from WWI platinum shortages to the loss of Mountain Pass rare-earth dominance (1965–1995) and near-total US import reliance on chromium, cobalt and manganese by 1983. Data. Historical synthesis (WWI–1990s) using case studies, import-reliance figures and stockpile valuations ($1.6B–$7.7B across eras).
  • Vital Signs 2026: The Health and Readiness of the Defense Industrial Base · National Defense Industrial Association (2026) NDIA's annual defense-industrial-base health assessment, including supply-chain findings. Summary. NDIA's flagship annual assessment (April 2026) scores US defence-industrial-base health across eight dimensions — including production-inputs and supply-chain conditions — from a record 1,646 survey respondents, and offers 83 recommendations. A dedicated "Ensuring Resilient Supply Chains" pillar addresses critical-mineral dependence; 47% of government respondents cited supply-chain challenges as a major issue. Data. NDIA Vital Signs 2026 survey (1,646 government/industry respondents) plus quantitative "vital signs" indicators across eight dimensions.
  • Domestic Processing Capabilities of Critical Minerals — Request for Project Proposals (RPP-CM-26-01) · Defense Industrial Base Consortium (2026) DOD-affiliated consortium solicitation targeting domestic critical-minerals processing. Summary. Solicitation (released 27 Feb 2026, closed 20 Mar 2026) from the Defense Industrial Base Consortium under the Office of Industrial Base Policy. It funds domestic critical-minerals processing capacity through two authorities — Industrial Base Analysis and Sustainment (IBAS) and Defense Production Act Title III (DPAP) — via equity, loans and offtake, to reduce reliance on foreign sources. Data. Government solicitation document — funding mechanism and eligibility criteria, not a dataset.
  • Defense Industrial Base Consortium Issues New Critical Minerals Request for Project Proposals · Inside Government Contracts (2026) Coverage of the DIBC's second critical-minerals RPP round and its target-material list. Summary. Coverage of the DIBC's second critical-minerals solicitation (27 Feb 2026, closed 20 Mar 2026), seeking proposals across supply-chain stages from extraction to recycling for thirteen defence-critical minerals — arsenic, bismuth, gadolinium, germanium, graphite, hafnium, nickel, samarium, tungsten, vanadium, ytterbium, yttrium and zirconium — under DPA Title III and 10 U.S.C. §4817, with awards of $100M–$500M. Data. Trade-press summary of the DIBC solicitation; no primary dataset.

Across the chain — criticality frameworks & policy 1

The list methodology that classifies the whole chain's inputs, cross-checked against the material set.
  • Study on the Critical Raw Materials for the EU 2023 · European Commission (2023) The EU's formal CRM criticality assessment, cross-checked for the chain's material list. Summary. European Commission page: the 2023 (fifth) criticality assessment screens 70 candidate materials on Economic Importance × Supply Risk and lists 34 critical raw materials plus strategic copper and nickel, feeding the Critical Raw Materials Act. Concentration examples: China supplies 100% of the EU's heavy rare earths, Turkey 99% of boron, South Africa 71% of platinum. Data. EU criticality methodology (Economic Importance × Supply Risk) over 70 candidate materials using production, trade, substitution and recycling data.

Across the chain — trade & commodity data 1

The dataset the chain's trade lens is built from — read as availability of the traded good, not who arms whom.
  • CEPII BACI V202601, based on UN Comtrade · CEPII Harmonized bilateral trade database (HS6, ~1995–2024) used for the chain's trade table. Summary. CEPII database (version 202601, released 30 Jan 2026): BACI provides reconciled bilateral trade flows for ~200 countries across ~5,000 HS6 products, derived from UN Comtrade by converting CIF import values to FOB and reliability-weighting reporter discrepancies. Its shared HS baskets (gallium and germanium sit together) are why customs data cannot see military end-use. Data. UN Comtrade import/export declarations, HS6 bilateral, mirror-reconciled (CIF/FOB, reporter-reliability weighting).
