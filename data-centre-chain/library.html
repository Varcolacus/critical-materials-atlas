Research Library · Data centres

Data-centre / AI-compute chain

A curated reference library of public sources for deeper study — gathered August 2026. Each entry links to a real source and carries an abstract (or factual summary) plus the data it rests on. A research-support layer of the Critical Materials Atlas.

The story of AI infrastructure is told as a chip story, and the chip is a genuine chokepoint. But the constraint that now bites is electricity: data-centre power demand is set to roughly double by 2030, and connecting that load to the grid — transformers, substations, interconnection queues — is the binding limit. The wall is megawatts, not a mineral.

Ordered to follow the chain: ① chips & hardware (the upstream chokepoint) → ② electricity demand → ③ grid connection: transformers & substations → ④ cooling, copper & the material build-out → ⑤ operators — then the cross-cutting frameworks and trade data that sit across every stage.

① Chips & AI hardware — the upstream chokepoint 5

The chip is a real chokepoint — but it is analysed in its own chain; here it is the starting hardware the load is built from.
  • SEMI, Semiconductor Manufacturing Monitor · SEMI Quarterly semiconductor manufacturing / fab-capacity data covering the chip layer upstream of data centres. Summary. SEMI's quarterly Semiconductor Manufacturing Monitor tracks fab utilisation, equipment and materials spending, installed capacity and shipment trends across the global chipmaking supply chain. (Subscription market-data product; landing page not directly retrievable — HTTP 403; summary from the product description.) Data. Aggregated SEMI member / industry survey data on fab operations and equipment–materials markets.
  • SEMI, Market Intelligence · SEMI SEMI's market-data hub for the semiconductor and electronics supply chain (3,000+ member companies). Summary. SEMI's Market Intelligence portfolio compiles semiconductor market research, forecasts and data reports spanning equipment, materials and fab activity across the chip supply chain. (Association market-data hub; page not directly retrievable — HTTP 403; summary from title and context.) Data. Association-compiled industry statistics and forecasts.
  • USGS, Key Minerals in Data Centers Infographic · USGS USGS map of the mineral commodities embedded in data-centre hardware. Summary. USGS infographic mapping the minerals used to build and run data centres by component: server boards/circuitry (silver, gold, copper, tin, tantalum, palladium, platinum), heat sinks (aluminium, copper), semiconductors (arsenic, fluorite, gallium, germanium, indium, silicon, tantalum) and magnets/storage (barite, boron, rare earths); notes data-centre load roughly tripled 2014–2024. Data. US import-reliance figures from USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 (gallium/germanium/indium/tantalum 100%, rare earths 80%).
  • Life-Cycle Emissions of AI Hardware: A Cradle-To-Grave Approach and Generational Trends · arXiv (2025) Cradle-to-grave manufacturing/operation analysis for data-centre accelerators. Abstract. A cradle-to-grave greenhouse-gas assessment of five generations of accelerator hardware in data centres — raw-material extraction, manufacturing, operation and disposal. Operational electricity dominates (~70–90% of lifecycle emissions); memory is the largest embodied-carbon driver (about a third); carbon per inference improved roughly threefold across four years. Data. Direct fleet measurement (power sampled every five minutes) over an assumed six-year lifespan under the GHG Protocol (Scopes 1–3), with market-, location- and hourly carbon-free-energy accounting.
  • The Energy Cost of the A.I. Lifecycle in Communication Networks · arXiv (2024) Energy-consumption formulas across the machine-learning lifecycle in network / data-centre contexts. Abstract. A lifecycle energy analysis of deploying machine-learning workloads across communication networks — development, training, inference and network transmission. Finds energy costs extend well beyond training, with inference and data-transmission overhead contributing substantially, and that architecture choice materially affects cumulative energy; offers accuracy-versus-energy trade-off frameworks. Data. Lifecycle assessment combining empirical power measurements with theoretical modelling across architectures (Transformers, CNNs, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks).

② Electricity demand — the curve that changed the problem 9

Roughly a doubling of data-centre electricity by 2030 — the demand that moved the chokepoint downstream.
  • IEA, Energy and AI — Energy demand from AI · IEA (2025) The central IEA analysis of AI-driven data-centre electricity demand; source of the 485→950 TWh projection. Abstract. IEA analysis: data-centre electricity was about 415 TWh in 2024 (~1.5% of world electricity), growing ~12% a year. In the base case it doubles to around 945 TWh by 2030 (just under 3% of world electricity), with the United States and China accounting for nearly 80% of the growth. Data. Scenario-based approach (Lift-Off, High Efficiency, Headwinds cases) built on near-term industry projections for server shipments.
  • IEA, Energy and AI — Executive summary · IEA (2025) Top-line findings of the flagship IEA Energy and AI report. Abstract. The flagship report examines the two-way link between energy and AI. Data centres used about 1.5% of world electricity in 2024 (415 TWh), led by the US (45%), China (25%) and Europe (15%); consumption more than doubles to ~945 TWh by 2030, renewables meeting half the growth, with wide uncertainty (700–1,700 TWh by 2035). Data. A new global model and dataset of data-centre electricity demand, plus consultation with policymakers, the technology sector and the energy industry.
  • IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI — Executive summary · IEA (2026) Follow-up IEA report addressing open questions on data-centre energy. Abstract. A 2026 follow-up updating the earlier landmark analysis. Capital expenditure of just five technology firms now exceeds global oil-and-gas production investment; per-task efficiency improves at least an order of magnitude annually, yet demanding applications use far more per query. Data-centre electricity roughly doubles from 485 TWh (2025) to 950 TWh (2030). Data. Satellite-based tracking of data-centre development, financial-market analysis, economic modelling with OECD cooperation, and an energy-company survey on adoption barriers.
  • IEA, AI is set to drive surging electricity demand from data centres · IEA (2025) News release accompanying the Energy and AI report, with the 2030/2035 scenario range (700–1,700 TWh). Summary. News release (10 April 2025) for the Energy and AI report: worldwide data-centre electricity demand more than doubles by 2030 to about 945 TWh, with demand from AI-optimised facilities more than quadrupling; US data centres account for almost half of national demand growth to 2030. Data. Comprehensive global analysis of energy–AI links, spanning renewables and natural gas as supply options.
  • IEA, Energy supply for AI · IEA (2025) IEA analysis of how data-centre power demand will be met (renewables, nuclear, gas). Abstract. Electricity generation serving data centres rises from 460 TWh (2024) to over 1,000 TWh by 2030 and 1,300 TWh by 2035 (base case). The 2024 mix is roughly coal 30%, renewables 27%, natural gas 26%, nuclear 15%; renewables meet nearly half of added demand, growing 22% a year; CO₂ emissions peak near 320 Mt around 2030. Data. Analysis of the fuel mix physically consumed by data centres, rather than contractual renewable agreements.
  • US DOE / LBNL, 2024 Report on U.S. Data Center Energy Use (announcement) · US DOE (2024) DOE announcement of the LBNL report; source of the US 58→176 TWh (2014→2023) history. Summary. DOE announcement of Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's 2024 report: US data-centre electricity rose from 58 TWh (2014) to 176 TWh (2023) and is projected at 325–580 TWh by 2028 (roughly 6.7–12% of US electricity, up from 4.4% in 2023). DOE outlines onsite-power, retired-coal-site and advanced-nuclear responses. Data. Based on the LBNL 2024 US data-centre energy usage report projecting consumption through 2028.
  • 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report (full PDF) · LBNL / US DOE (2024) The underlying report with the full 2014–2028 US series and the 2028 scenario band. Abstract. The LBNL report, mandated by the Energy Act of 2020, reconstructs US data-centre electricity back to 2014 and projects to 2028. Use held near 60 TWh through 2014–2016, then climbed as accelerated servers grew, reaching 176 TWh (4.4%) by 2023; scenarios span 325–580 TWh (6.7–12%) by 2028, or 74–132 GW of power demand. Data. Bottom-up model of server, storage, network and infrastructure stock; input data from Omdia, Dell'Oro Group, S&P Global and IDC, reviewed by ~50 industry organisations (LBNL-2001637).
  • Electricity Demand and Grid Impacts of AI Data Centers · arXiv (2025) Review of demand patterns across training / inference and the resulting grid impacts. Abstract. A review of how fast-growing data-centre loads strain power grids, covering infrastructure and demand patterns from model preparation through inference. Frames the challenge across three timescales — long-term planning and interconnection, short-term markets and operations, and real-time stability — urging coordination among grid operators, facility operators and end users. Data. Literature review and synthesis of existing studies (no new empirical dataset), spanning grid, data-centre and end-user perspectives.
  • Power for AI Data Centers: Energy Demand, Grid Impacts, Challenges and Perspectives · MDPI Energies Peer-reviewed review of data-centre power demand and grid-integration challenges. Summary. Peer-reviewed review of the electricity demand of data centres, their impacts on power grids, and mitigation perspectives. (Full text not directly retrievable — HTTP 403 on the MDPI page and its full-text mirror; noted from the article's title and listing.) Data. Peer-reviewed review article (MDPI Energies).

③ The grid connection — transformers & substations 4

The binding limit: transformers with multi-year lead times, substation capacity and interconnection queues — the same wall the grid chain documents.
  • ChinaTalk, Gridlocked: Transformer Shortage Choking U.S. Supply Chains · ChinaTalk Deep dive on the grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) bottleneck behind the transformer shortage. Abstract. A critical shortage of electrical transformers is obstructing US infrastructure across housing, data centres and renewables. Domestic supply meets only ~20% of demand; lead times rose from 50 weeks (2021) to 120 weeks (2024); prices climbed 60–80% since 2020. The authors urge CHIPS-Act-style industrial policy and a strategic transformer reserve. Data. Wood Mackenzie lead-time data (Apr 2024), a NIAC June 2024 supply-side report, transformer trade figures and utility resource-planner interviews.
  • PowerMag, Transformers in 2026: Shortage, Scramble, or Self-Inflicted Crisis? · Power Magazine (2026) Industry analysis of the 2026 transformer shortage and its causes. Summary. Examines whether the US transformer shortage is a genuine supply crisis or a procurement problem. Wood Mackenzie projects 30% power and 10% distribution shortfalls through 2025; lead times reach 128–144 weeks; prices are up 45–77% since 2019 — though one market broker disputes that any true shortage exists. Data. Wood Mackenzie August 2025 projections, Q2 2025 lead-time surveys, manufacturing-investment tracking and broker interviews.
  • The Conversation, Supply-chain delays, rising equipment prices threaten electricity grid · The Conversation Explainer on equipment-price and lead-time pressure on grid buildout. Abstract. Equipment shortages — not generation or transmission capacity — are the critical bottleneck constraining US grid expansion. Transformer lead times reached about 120 weeks (up to four years for large units) at four-to-six times pre-2022 cost; circuit-breaker waits doubled to nearly three years; some gas turbines are sold out seven years ahead. Data. NREL transformer-inventory data, NERC reliability assessments, Duke Nicholas Institute research citing Wood Mackenzie, and DOE reports.
  • Rabobank, Supply chain constraints are curbing US data center development · Rabobank Financial-sector analysis of power, water, materials and labour constraints on US data-centre buildout. Summary. Financial-sector analysis of how power, water, materials and labour constraints are curbing US data-centre development. (Page hard-blocked — HTTP 403 to both automated fetch and browser-headed retrieval; noted from the article title only.) Data. Rabobank research note — no primary dataset.

④ Cooling, copper & the material build-out 3

A data centre is copper, water and steel — a large demand, but no single bulk material is a unique chokepoint. The honest framing is energy-and-chips, not a new mineral.
  • Generative AI impact assessment through a life cycle analysis of multiple data center typologies · ScienceDirect (2025) LCA of generative workloads across differing data-centre designs, at up to 130 kW/rack density. Summary. Life-cycle assessment of generative workloads across multiple data-centre typologies, modelling high-density infrastructure at up to 130 kW per rack. (Publisher abstract page not directly retrievable — HTTP 403 / paywall; noted from the article's title and indexing.) Data. Life-cycle assessment comparing multiple data-centre typologies (Applied Energy, 2025).
  • Fastmarkets, How AI and data center growth drive copper demand in the US · Fastmarkets Market analysis of copper demand from data-centre and AI buildout (busbars, cold plates for liquid cooling). Summary. AI and data-centre expansion are accelerating US copper demand: energy-transition copper use is projected to grow 8.9% a year through 2035, with Macquarie estimating 330,000–420,000 t of copper in data centres by 2030. Construction is 30–40% electrical, cooling an increasingly large driver (busbars, cold plates). Data. Fastmarkets 10-year outlook (May 2025), Macquarie research, a NEMA 2024 cooling study and industry-expert interviews.
  • Bank of America Institute, Data center construction creates a resource shock · BofA Institute Estimates data-centre-driven copper demand and metal intensity per MW. Abstract. Data-centre construction creates a resource shock across water, power and metals. Up to 75% of a data centre's water use occurs off-site through electricity generation; each incremental megawatt of capacity embeds roughly 60–75 tons of metals, especially copper. Global capacity is expected to exceed 200 GW by 2030 and triple by 2035. Data. BofA Global Research, drawing on IEA and BloombergNEF data, a Black & Veatch 2025 survey of 680 US water-sector stakeholders, and university water-demand estimates.

⑤ Operators & the wider picture 3

What the operators building now actually report — and the broad reviews that put the whole load in context.
  • Uptime Institute, Research and Reports · Uptime Institute The data-centre industry's principal research body; annual survey and infrastructure reports. Summary. Index of Uptime Institute Intelligence research: annual global data-centre surveys (2018–2026), outage analyses, year-ahead predictions and topic reports on direct liquid cooling, energy efficiency, sustainability, staffing and resiliency. Executive summaries are public; fuller reports require membership. Data. Uptime Institute Intelligence publications catalogue spanning 2017–2026.
  • Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey Results 2025 · Uptime Institute (2025) 800+-operator survey finding power availability is now a top-tier strategic constraint. Abstract. The 15th annual Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey describes a resilient industry now facing rising costs, worsening power constraints and difficulty meeting AI-driven demand. Operators must simultaneously manage availability, efficiency, staffing shortages and supply-chain delays as facilities expand for power and density. Data. 15th annual global operator survey; a 13-page public executive summary excerpting a 31-page full report.
  • Reviewing the socio-technical dynamics of AI, data centers and digitalization on energy and the environment · ScienceDirect (2026) Broad review connecting AI / data-centre growth to energy-system and environmental effects. Abstract. A socio-technical review screening 364 articles (2000–2025) across four dimensions of AI, data centres and digitalisation: natural resources, facilities and components, applications, and users and institutions. It asks which low- and zero-carbon technologies mitigate energy and carbon footprints, what blocks adoption, and which policies help. Data. Systematic literature review of 364 peer-reviewed articles (2000–2025); Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews.

Across the chain — criticality frameworks & policy 3

List methodologies and US assessments that classify the hardware layer's mineral inputs.
  • USGS, Department of the Interior releases draft 2025 List of Critical Minerals · USGS (2025) The current US critical-minerals list, relevant to the chip/hardware layer of the chain. Summary. The Interior Department, through the USGS, released a draft 2025 Critical Minerals List of 54 commodities — recommending adding potash, silicon, copper, silver, rhenium and lead, and removing arsenic and tellurium — to guide federal strategy, investment and permitting. Data. USGS economic-effects model assessing 1,200+ trade-disruption scenarios over 84 commodities and 402 industries; probability-weighted economic impact.
  • CRS R47982, Critical Mineral Resources: National Policy and Critical Minerals List · Congressional Research Service Background on the US critical-minerals list and import reliance (100% reliant for 12 of 50 minerals). Abstract. The report reviews changes to national materials and minerals policy, the evolving definition of a "critical mineral," and development of the critical-minerals list, then discusses the list's impact on federal policy. It notes the 2021 amendment to the 1980 policy act and the supply-resilience issues facing Congress. Data. CRS report R47982 (updated Jan 2026); legal/policy analysis grounded in the National Materials and Minerals Policy Act and Energy Act of 2020.
  • CRS R48005, Critical Mineral Resources: The USGS Role in Research and Analysis · Congressional Research Service How USGS assesses and tracks critical-mineral supply for downstream industries including electronics. Abstract. The report examines the USGS as the lead federal science agency for mineral-resource research. Under the Energy Act of 2020 the Secretary of the Interior, through the USGS Director, designates critical minerals as those essential to economic and national security with vulnerable supply chains and essential manufacturing functions; it covers national mapping and data initiatives. Data. CRS report R48005 (Feb 2025, Linda R. Rowan); USGS mineral-resource research and import-reliance data.

Across the chain — trade & commodity data 4

The datasets the chain's own figures are built from — and the trade caveat that a data centre is built and operated in place.
  • CEPII BACI V202601, based on UN Comtrade · CEPII Harmonised bilateral trade database (HS6, 1995–2024) used for the chain's hardware trade table. Summary. BACI provides bilateral trade flows for ~200 countries across ~5,000 HS6 products. It reconciles duplicate Comtrade reports in two steps — estimating and removing CIF costs to align importer with FOB exporter values, then down-weighting reporters whose data diverge from partners to set harmonised flows. Data. Reconciled bilateral trade from UN Comtrade; HS6, 1995–2024; version V202601 (released 30 Jan 2026), Etalab 2.0 licence.

The LBNL Energy Technologies Area publication record for the 2024 US Data Center Energy Usage Report — the repository entry for the report's dataset and methodology. (Landing page not directly retrievable — HTTP 403; the report PDF it hosts was read directly and appears above at #12.) Data. Publication record for the LBNL 2024 report (LBNL-2001637).
  • DCD, IEA: Data center energy consumption set to double by 2030 to 945TWh · Data Center Dynamics (2025) Trade-press summary of the IEA projection. Summary. Trade-press summary of the IEA projection: global data-centre electricity use more than doubles to 945 TWh by 2030 from 415 TWh in 2024, with AI the main driver and the US and China together accounting for nearly 80% of the growth.   • Carbon Brief, AI: Five charts that put data-centre energy use — and emissions — into context · Carbon Brief Chart-driven explainer contextualising data-centre energy and emissions growth.
