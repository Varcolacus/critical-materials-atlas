# Aerospace-chain interpretation notes

## The claim the pilot makes

For jet propulsion, a critical-minerals list read at the mine misidentifies the
risk. The binding constraints sit downstream — in a scarce by-product (rhenium),
in *qualification* rather than tonnage (aerospace titanium), in a casting
capability held by a few firms, and finally in a three-firm engine oligopoly.

## Claims rejected

- **"China controls aerospace titanium because it makes most sponge."** Rejected.
  Sponge tonnage is not aerospace-qualified supply. The qualified aerospace base
  is Japan, Russia and Kazakhstan; the US imports ~80% of its sponge from Japan.
  The pilot shows production and qualification as two different measures.
- **"Rhenium is a mining chokepoint like any other."** Partly rejected. Rhenium
  is a chokepoint, but by *by-product coupling and scarcity*, not by refining
  concentration — no mine produces it as a primary product, so its ~81 t/yr
  cannot be scaled without scaling copper. That is a different kind of risk.
- **"Engine export shares show who makes engines."** Rejected. Turbojet customs
  headings are lumpy project trade and miss captive OEM transfers; the maker
  structure is company market share (GE / P&W / RR), not trade share.
- **A single-crystal-casting country series.** Not attempted. No public annual
  country-production series exists; the stage is described by company control and
  labelled qualitative.

## Rules reused (from the chain method)

1. Define the physical transformation before choosing a dataset.
2. Prefer production or capacity for "who makes it."
3. Show ownership separately when a few firms — not countries — hold the stage.
4. Use customs data only at the specificity the HS code actually has; rhenium has
   no clean line, so it is read from production, not trade.
5. Put years, units, scope and forecast status beside every measure.
6. Show gaps (single-crystal casting) rather than invent a series.
