Research Library · Medical Isotopes

Medical-isotopes chain (Mo-99 / Tc-99m)

A curated reference library of public sources for deeper study — gathered August 2026. Each entry links to a real source and carries an abstract (or factual summary) plus the data it rests on. A research-support layer of the Critical Materials Atlas.

Technetium-99m images tens of millions of patients a year, but the chain that delivers it races a clock: its parent molybdenum-99 is made in about six ageing research reactors and decays with a 66-hour half-life, so none of it can be stockpiled. The chokepoint here is not a hostile supplier — it is fragility.

Ordered to follow the chain: ① research reactors (few, ageing) → ② Mo-99 (66-hour half-life, no stockpile) → ③ Tc-99m generators → ④ hospital scans — then the cross-cutting supply-security frameworks and trade data that sit across every stage.

① Research reactors — the ageing production base 2

A handful of research reactors, most built in the 1960s–70s, irradiate the targets that become Mo-99.
  • NNSA's Molybdenum-99 Program — a reliable supply produced without highly enriched uranium · US NNSA (2024) US program to establish a domestic Mo-99 supply produced without highly enriched uranium (HEU). Summary. NNSA's Mo-99 Program minimises civilian highly-enriched-uranium use — helping global producers convert to low-enriched uranium and funding domestic US Mo-99 made without HEU. Mo-99 and its decay product Tc-99m serve over 40,000 US medical procedures daily; a SHINE plant under construction targets over 75% of US demand. Data. US government program page; cooperative agreements with commercial producers (SHINE, NorthStar) plus reactor-conversion and uranium-supply data — no dataset served on the page.
  • The necessity of nuclear reactors for targeted radionuclide therapies · Trends in Biotechnology (2013) Argues nuclear reactors remain essential for Tc-99m/I-131 and for targeted radionuclide cancer therapy. Abstract. Reactors are required for the workhorses of diagnosis and treatment — Tc-99m and I-131 — and remain essential to the expanding field of targeted radionuclide cancer therapy. The main reactors in the global supply are ageing and expected to shut before 2025; medicine, nuclear industry and politics must secure future reactor access and industrialise alternative production routes. Data. Review / opinion (Krijger, Ponsard, Harfensteller et al.) — synthesis of the reactor-supply outlook, no primary dataset.

② Mo-99 — the isotope that races the clock 4

Extracted and shipped within days: a 66-hour half-life means no buffer can exist, so any outage bites at once.
  • IAEA — production and supply of medical radioisotopes · IAEA (2024) IAEA topic page on the production, supply and application of medical radioisotopes. Abstract. IAEA topic hub on producing and supplying medical radioisotopes for diagnosis and therapy. (Page not retrievable this pass — repeated HTTP 402 Payment Required; scope taken from the page title and its role as a chain source, no figures quoted.) Data. IAEA agency topic page — descriptive hub, no dataset served on the page.
  • Current Trends in the Supply and Utilisation of Medical Radioisotopes · OECD NEA (2025) NEA assessment of supply/utilisation trends across Mo-99 and emerging therapeutic isotopes. Summary. NEA report assessing the supply and utilisation of medical radioisotopes — from established Mo-99 challenges to emerging radioligand therapy and theranostics. Drawing on 15+ years of work, it reviews production capacity, innovation pipelines, regulation and health-system readiness, with strategic policy recommendations for sustainable access. Data. NEA synthesis over 15+ years of supply monitoring; report landing page (full technical detail in the PDF).
  • The Security of Supply of Medical Radioisotopes: Demand and Capacity Projections for 99Mo/99mTc, 2023–2027 · OECD NEA (2023) NEA demand/capacity projections for 99Mo/99mTc across the 2023–2027 window. Summary. NEA report (NEA/NDC/R(2023)1, Oct 2023) giving security-of-supply scenarios to 2027 — the first since 2019 — comparing demand estimates against production-capacity and facility-utilisation forecasts, using confidential supply-chain data, to guide policymaker and producer decisions on sustainable Mo-99/Tc-99m supply. Data. NEA modelling using confidential participant data on demand, capacity and reactor utilisation, 2023–2027.
  • Molybdenum-99/technetium-99m management: race against time · Annals of Nuclear Medicine (2011) Short letter on the fragility of Tc-99m supply and its short-half-life constraint. Abstract. Molybdenum-99 is the parent of diagnostic nuclear medicine, decaying to technetium-99m used in over 30 million investigations a year worldwide. Supplies of Tc-99m have stayed fragile in recent years and may be again in the short and long term; the letter registers suggestions to cope with inadequate supply of this most-wanted radionuclide. Data. Letter (M. Ahmad) — commentary and suggestions, no primary dataset.

③ Tc-99m generators — parent to tracer 1

Mo-99 decays to technetium-99m inside a hospital "moly cow" generator — and the routes that feed it are being remade off weapons uranium.
  • Sustained availability of 99mTc: possible paths forward · Journal of Nuclear Medicine (2013) Reviews reactor- and accelerator-based routes to sustain Tc-99m supply without HEU. Abstract. Tc-99m availability rests on 99Mo/99mTc generators made from fission Mo-99 produced with high-enriched-uranium targets now being phased out for proliferation reasons, with no plans to replace the limited reactor fleet. Reviews reactor- and accelerator-based non-HEU routes — their advantages, technical challenges, economics, security and regulatory obstacles — and the international efforts underway. Data. Review (Pillai, Dash, Knapp) — synthesis of production technologies and international programmes, no primary dataset.

④ The scan — clinical demand 1

Tc-99m is injected as a tracer for bone, cardiac, renal and cancer imaging — roughly 80% of nuclear-medicine procedures.
  • Radioisotopes in Medicine · World Nuclear Association (2026) Industry-body overview of Tc-99m's diagnostic dominance and the Mo-99 reactor base. Summary. WNA overview: technetium-99m accounts for about 80% of nuclear-medicine procedures and 85% of diagnostic scans; over 50 million nuclear-medicine procedures occur yearly (~20 million in the USA). Mo-99 (66-hour half-life) comes from a limited set of reactors; accelerator routes cost three-to-ten times more. Data. Industry-body synthesis citing a producer-reactor capacity table (~26,440 six-day Ci of Mo-99) and procedure statistics.

Across the chain — supply-security frameworks & policy 2

The programmes and policy principles built to keep a buffer-less supply chain from failing.
  • The Supply of Medical Radioisotopes (Mo-99/Tc-99m) — NEA programme hub · OECD NEA (2023) NEA medical-radioisotope programme hub — the HLG-MR security-of-supply work. Abstract. NEA's medical-radioisotope programme hub. The High-Level Group on the Security of Supply of Medical Radioisotopes (HLG-MR) completed four mandates, identifying root causes of supply instability and proposing resilience principles. Mo-99 supply stays vulnerable — few producers, ageing facilities, geopolitics; the NEA now convenes supply workshops and tracks availability, extending focus to Lu-177 and Ac-225. Data. NEA programme page (the med-radio landing redirects here); HLG-MR findings and workshop outputs, no dataset served.
  • The Supply of Medical Radioisotopes: the HLG-MR policy approach · IAEA / OECD-NEA (2013) Documents the HLG-MR policy approach and full-cost-recovery methodology for Mo-99 supply. Summary. OECD/NEA self-assessment of the Mo-99/Tc-99m supply chain against the HLG-MR policy approach. Its central principle is full-cost recovery — pricing that recovers the full cost of irradiation to end host-nation subsidies — set out through six policy principles and periodic supply-chain-participant self-assessments. Data. OECD/NEA policy self-assessment (Peykov & Cameron); costing methodology and participant reporting, no primary dataset.

Across the chain — trade & commodity data 1

The dataset behind the chain's trade overlay — and why customs statistics barely see this material.
  • CEPII BACI V202601, based on UN Comtrade · CEPII Harmonized bilateral trade database; radioisotopes sit in one broad HS line (284440). Summary. CEPII's BACI provides reconciled bilateral trade flows for ~200 countries across ~5,000 HS6 products, 1995–2024, derived from UN Comtrade by removing CIF costs and reliability-weighting reporter discrepancies. Radioactive isotopes fall in one broad line (HS 284440), so Mo-99/Tc-99m cannot be isolated. Data. UN Comtrade import/export declarations, HS6 bilateral, mirror-reconciled (CIF/FOB, reporter-reliability weighting).

Gaps & good next searches

  • The 2009–2010 Tc-99m shortage — Canada's NRU (Chalk River) and the Netherlands' HFR (Petten) both down at once — is a well-documented case study but was not captured here with a citable primary source. Next: "2009 molybdenum-99 shortage NRU HFR" for contemporaneous reporting and post-mortems.
  • No dedicated public Mo-99/Tc-99m production database surfaced, and BACI's HS 284440 lumps all radioactive isotopes, so trade cannot isolate the medical series. Try the IAEA's nuclear-medicine and isotope databases (e.g. NUCLEUS).
  • Academic supply-economics work on the HEU→LEU transition is known to exist but had no confirmed live URL this pass — revisit Von Hippel & Kahn, "Molybdenum-99 production without highly enriched uranium," Science & Global Security (2006) directly.
  • No radiopharmaceutical-specific trade association was captured beyond the general WNA — search "Nuclear Medicine Europe" (formerly AIPES) and the SNMMI for sector-specific supply commentary.
  • Newer non-fission and accelerator routes (NorthStar, SHINE, TRIUMF) deserve a dedicated follow-up for company-level and DOE-program detail.
