The atlas in real tonnes

Every other page works in shares or trade value. This one brings in absolute physical production — and two more independently compiled sources. World Mining Data (Austrian ministry) gives mine output in metric tonnes; the British Geological Survey’s World Mineral Statistics adds a third count. Laid beside the atlas’s USGS-derived shares they do two things at once: show the scale nobody sees, and triangulate whether the producer geography holds up when two other compilations count it separately — and, where they disagree, let the two independents adjudicate.

The three sources, and how the check works

Absolute production: World Mining Data 6.4 (2026 ed.), production by country in metric tonnes, 2024. For each material we take the world total and the top producer’s tonnage share, and compare that share against the atlas’s existing USGS-derived top-producer share (in data.json). A third source, the British Geological Survey’s World Mineral Statistics (via its free OGC API, latest year per commodity), gives an independent third count for 18 of the materials. Same top country across sources + a small share gap = independently compiled compilations agreeing.

How independent is “independent”? We counted, because someone attacked this claim. The obvious objection to any second-source check is that the second source is just repackaging the first. World Mining Data tags every figure with where it came from, so the objection is answerable rather than arguable. Across 1,903 tagged figures in the 2024 edition: national statistics 60.0%, company reports 28.5%, questionnaire 6.7%, IEA 1.5%, ICG 1.1%, USGS 0.8% (16 figures), BP 0.7%, Kimberley 0.6%, WNA 0.2%. So WMD is not a repackaging of USGS — the circularity charge fails as usually put.

But the weaker version of the objection is right, and it bounds what this page can claim. Both compilations ultimately rest on the same upstream: national statistical returns and company reports. They are independent compilations, not independent measurements. If a country misreports its output, both inherit the error identically and agree perfectly — agreement would then be evidence of nothing. So what 26/28 demonstrates is compilation reliability: two teams, working separately from the same primary returns, made the same call. That is worth something and it is not nothing, but it is not measurement validation, and this page no longer says it is. No open source independently measures mine output. The closest thing the atlas owns is the satellite footprint layer — and it sees area, not tonnes. But area is measured from orbit, so it owes nothing to any national statistic — which makes it the one genuinely independent check available, if only a coarse one.

So we ran it. Does a country’s share of the world’s mapped mine footprint track its share of bulk open-pit output? This can only corroborate the aggregate mining geography — the polygons are undifferentiated coal/metal/aggregate, and area-per-tonne varies by orders of magnitude, so it cannot validate any per-metal share. But for the coarse claim “these are the world’s big mining nations,” it is the one check that does not rest on the same national returns as USGS and WMD — and it agrees.

Caveats: the sources define commodities slightly differently (e.g. contained-metal vs concentrate, ore vs oxide), report different years’ vintages, and treat re-processing differently — so a few points of share difference is expected, and the genuine disagreements are flagged, not hidden. Notably, on bauxite and baryte the two independents (World Mining Data and BGS) agree with each other and put a different country on top than the atlas’s USGS figure (Guinea for bauxite, China for baryte) — a case where the third source adjudicates rather than rubber-stamps; titanium splits three ways on the ilmenite-vs-slag definition. Coverage: 28 of 32 materials have a WMD sheet; BGS World Mineral Statistics gives a recent (≥2020) mine series for 18 of them (no BGS mine series for the pure by-products gallium, germanium, niobium, tantalum, PGMs). Sources: world-mining-data.info · BGS World Mineral Statistics → production.json.

What this changes for the atlas

This is the acquisition the whole session kept pointing to. With absolute tonnages the derived layers can finally speak in quantities: a cascade shock becomes “so many tonnes of world gallium”, not an index; the demand arm can be normalised to physical supply. And the cross-check earns trust the honest way — two more independently compiled authorities (World Mining Data and the British Geological Survey) put the same country on top for of the materials all three can check, so the producer geography the atlas has asserted all along is not a USGS artefact. Where they disagree, that is now visible and named, not smoothed over — and on bauxite and baryte the two independents even agree against USGS, which is exactly the kind of signal a triangulation is supposed to surface. This is the step from demonstration toward instrument.
