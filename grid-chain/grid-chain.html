The bottleneck is often the component, not the ore

The world has built power networks for more than a century, yet the next expansion is comparable in length to today’s entire grid. Copper and aluminium matter—but conductor architecture, electrical steel, cable plants, transformer capacity and project delivery determine where the constraint actually sits.

metal production ≠ grid demand ≠ equipment output ≠ investment ≠ customs trade

The correction. “The grid needs copper” is true but incomplete. Aluminium dominates many overhead conductors; copper remains valuable where compactness and conductivity matter; and specialised transformers or cables can be scarce even when bulk metal is available.

Copper and aluminium are design choices

Copper

Compact, highly conductive

More conductivity per area

Useful where space, losses, joints and compactness dominate. It is central in many cables, windings and substations, but weight and cost work against it in long overhead spans.

Aluminium

Light, larger cross-section

~2× current by equal weight

Its lower density favours overhead lines. Lower conductivity per area means more cross-section, and steel reinforcement or advanced cores supply mechanical strength.

No universal substitution ratio. Route, voltage, sag, strength, diameter, connections, losses, installation and lifetime cost all affect the choice. Overhead, underground and submarine systems are different engineering problems.

Transformers expose the manufacturing bottleneck

IEA evidence shows that procurement pressure now sits years upstream of energisation. This is not reducible to copper tonnage: grain-oriented electrical steel, transposed wire, insulation, design and factory test capacity are all part of the product.
