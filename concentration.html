Supply structure · concentration over time

Two materials carry the critical-minerals concentration story

Most critical materials did grow more concentrated between 1995–2004 and 2015–2024 — the typical one modestly, and by more than comparable ordinary commodities. But the large average that usually gets cited is almost entirely lithium and cobalt: drop those two and it falls from +0.034 to essentially zero. So the alarming version of the story, in production terms, is mostly a story about two materials. Built from production data alone — no trade, no consumption model.

The number to quote is the median, not the mean. The typical critical material became modestly more concentrated (median +0.046), and it did so while the typical comparable ordinary commodity did not. That result is robust: strip out the two biggest movers and the median barely moves. The mean tells a louder story — but a false one. It is +0.034 only because two materials sit at the far edge of the distribution; remove them and it collapses. Anyone citing “critical minerals are concentrating” from an average is, on these numbers, mostly citing lithium and cobalt.

Is it a class, or is it two materials?

The honest answer is both, at different volumes. As a class, critical materials lean toward concentration: 15 of 23 rose, and the typical one by a modest +0.046. But the dramatic, headline-grade concentration is two materials — the battery-cathode pair. Lithium’s HHI more than tripled (0.21 → 0.71) and cobalt’s tripled too (0.14 → 0.45). And it is not a clean “energy transition” story: two of the most-discussed transition metals moved the other way — graphite (−0.19) and rare earths (−0.18) came off near-monopoly highs as new suppliers entered. So “critical minerals are concentrating” is true of the typical material only mildly, and true dramatically of two.

What counts as a control — and why most don’t

A control only works if it is comparable. Many ordinary minerals — gypsum, salt, aggregates, talc, perlite — deconcentrate for a reason that has nothing to do with supply risk: they are bulky and low-value, so they are produced next to where they are used. That is transport economics, not a counterfactual, and it would flatter the result. The genuinely comparable controls are the ones that share critical materials’ production logic — globally traded, mined where the geology is: gold, silver and potash. Their typical change was −0.037: no concentration. It is a small basket (three), and that smallness is itself telling — there are few important, globally-traded, non-critical commodities, because being scarce and concentrated is much of what gets a material called critical in the first place.

Two honesties this rests on

The level is partly circular; the change is the finding. Critical materials are more concentrated than ordinary ones in level (mean HHI 0.29 vs 0.19), but supply concentration is an explicit criterion for calling a material critical, so that gap is close to definitional. Nothing in any definition says a material must become more concentrated over time — that is what the change measures, and the baseline decade (1995–2004) predates the EU critical-raw-materials list (2011).

Selection bites through the change too, and lithium is the case. The critical lists were rewritten repeatedly between 2011 and 2023 — inside the window — and lithium was added partly because it concentrated. A full defence would freeze the classification to before the baseline; that is the outstanding robustness check. The partial defence is already visible above: the median survives removing lithium and cobalt, so the typical-material result does not depend on the recently-salient cases.

The coverage question, stated correctly. Concentration indices can rise simply because fewer countries report. Reporter counts actually rose in both groups on average — but that average does not clear a nonlinear, per-material index, and the single biggest mover cuts the other way: lithium’s reporting producers fell from 10 to 7 as its HHI tripled, so part of that one rise is thinner reporting, not deeper concentration. That is exactly why lithium and cobalt are shown as individual dots, not folded into a group average. Cobalt is the clean case — its producer count rose (14 → 20) while its HHI tripled.
What this rests on, and what it doesn’t. Concentration is measured on mine/primary production tonnages from the BGS World Mineral Statistics panel — one consistent source across every material, computed only for years with at least five reporting producers. It uses no trade data, no apparent-consumption estimate, and no model, so it is independent of everything still open in the atlas’s consumption layer. It measures where material is produced, not refined or consumed — a material can have diversified mines and a concentrated midstream (that is the chokepoint question, and a different measurement).
