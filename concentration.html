Supply structure · concentration over time

Ordinary commodities spread out. Critical materials don’t.

Over three decades, production of ordinary minerals became less concentrated as more countries dug in — the normal pattern. Critical materials broke it: most grew more concentrated. The test that makes this a finding rather than a slogan is the control group — 13 non-critical minerals, run the same way. Almost nobody publishing on critical minerals runs one. This is built from production data alone: no trade, no consumption model, no forecast.

The eye-catching number is the level: critical materials are more concentrated than ordinary ones (mean HHI 0.29 vs 0.19). Ignore it. That gap is partly circular — supply concentration is an explicit criterion for calling a material critical, so “criticals are concentrated” is close to definitional. The finding is the change. Nothing in any criticality definition says a material must become more concentrated over time. If criticality just meant “naturally rare and concentrated,” these materials would be flat and high — not rising. They rose while ordinary commodities fell, and the baseline decade (1995–2004) predates the EU critical-raw-materials list (2011) and most national lists entirely.

The confound an analyst checks first — and it runs the wrong way for us. HHI rises automatically when fewer countries report. But over this window both groups gained reporters — the critical group 23 → 26 producing countries per year on average, the control group 44 → 47. More countries were producing in each, yet criticals concentrated while controls spread out. So the criticals’ rise is despite more producers, not a reporting artifact. That is the single strongest line here, so it sits in the open, not a footnote.

Is it the energy transition? No — and that’s the interesting part

The obvious story would be that the materials the energy transition needs concentrated while the rest spread out. It doesn’t hold. The transition metals hold the two hardest concentrators — lithium (+0.50) and cobalt (+0.31), the battery-cathode pair — but they also hold the biggest diversifiers: graphite (−0.19) and rare earths (−0.18) came off near-monopoly highs as new suppliers entered. Split the criticals and the concentration is actually more consistent among the old industrial ones (10 of 14 rose) than the transition group (5 of 9). So this isn’t a story about batteries and magnets — it’s broad across critical materials, with the battery raw materials as the extreme edge, not the cause.

The exceptions, named

A finding you can only believe if the counter-cases are on the table. Seven criticals diversified: antimony (−0.34), graphite (−0.19), rare earths (−0.18), platinum-group metals (−0.10), barytes, tin and copper. The three biggest were all near-monopolies in the 1990s that have genuinely broadened since — real, and real evidence that concentration is not destiny. On the other side, only three ordinary minerals concentrated, and all barely — perlite (+0.04), silver (+0.02) and salt (+0.02), none on a shrinking producer set. Two commodities that would have concentrated were deliberately left out of the control group, not to flatter the result: diamond (a cartel-shaped market that behaves like a critical) and asbestos (whose producers vanished for regulatory reasons, a policy shock rather than market structure). Neither exclusion is needed for the divergence — 15 vs 10 pointing opposite ways — but naming them is.

What this rests on, and what it doesn’t. Concentration is measured on mine/primary production tonnages from the BGS World Mineral Statistics panel — a single, consistent source across all 36 materials and 30 years. HHI is the sum of squared national production shares, computed only for years with at least five reporting producers. This uses no trade data, no apparent-consumption estimate, and no model — which is why it is independent of everything still open in the atlas’s consumption layer. It measures where material is produced, not where it is refined or consumed; a material can have diversified mines and still a concentrated midstream (that is the chokepoint question, and a different measurement).
