Lead — the circular metal chain

A curated reference library of public sources for deeper study — gathered August 2026. Each entry links to a real source and carries an abstract (or factual summary) plus the data it rests on. A research-support layer of the Critical Materials Atlas.

Lead is the atlas's clearest circular story: more than half of all lead supply is recycled — overwhelmingly from spent lead-acid batteries — in a loop that closes locally, near demand. There is no geographic chokepoint; the binding constraint is toxicity, not scarcity.

Ordered to follow the chain: ① Primary ore (galena — China, Australia, Peru) → ② the recycling loop (~60% secondary lead, smelted locally) → ③ lead-acid batteries (~85% of use) → ④ the real risk: toxicity from informal recycling — then the market data, policy and trade sources that sit across every stage.

③ Lead-acid batteries & the circular economy 1

Where the lead goes — about 85% into lead-acid batteries — and why the loop is profitable and closed.
  • Lead-Acid Circular Economy: The World's Most Recycled Commodity · JYC Battery Industry commentary framing lead-acid as the world's most recycled commodity. Summary. Industry commentary: lead-acid batteries reach a ~99% recovery rate — the highest of any consumer product — because lead is infinitely recyclable, recovery is profitable without subsidy, and new batteries carry 60–80% recycled lead through mature closed-loop pyrometallurgical processing. Data. Market/industry commentary citing recovery-rate and recycled-content figures — no primary dataset.

④ The real risk — toxicity & informal recycling 9

Lead's binding constraint is health, not geography: informal, unregulated battery recycling poisons communities.
  • WHO — Lead poisoning and health · World Health Organization (2023) WHO fact sheet on lead's health effects and battery-recycling exposure. Abstract. WHO fact sheet: there is no safe blood-lead level; lead causes irreversible neurological harm in children and cardiovascular and kidney disease in adults. Lead-acid batteries are the largest use of lead, and informal recycling has caused mass-poisoning outbreaks. WHO attributes over 3.5 million deaths in 2023 to lead exposure. Data. WHO / IHME Global Burden of Disease estimates plus reviewed exposure literature.
  • Battery Recycling Is a Far Bigger Source of Lead Exposure Than We Thought · Center for Global Development CGD working-paper summary re-estimating battery recycling's share of lead exposure. Summary. CGD working paper: unsafe battery recycling drives roughly a third of lead exposure in lower-income countries (up from ~15%), because low-level contamination spreads up to 5 km from sites — on the order of 800,000 deaths and 180 million IQ points lost a year. Data. Extended geospatial exposure model over broad areas around recycling sites; DALY / mortality estimates.
  • Mass lead intoxication from informal used lead-acid battery recycling in Dakar, Senegal · PMC / Environmental Health Perspectives Landmark case study of mass lead poisoning from informal battery recycling. Abstract. Between Nov 2007 and Mar 2008 eighteen children died of lead encephalopathy in Thiaroye-sur-Mer, Dakar, after informal used-lead-acid-battery recycling contaminated soil to 302,000 mg/kg. Mean child blood-lead was 129.5 µg/dL (up to 613.9); 94% needed chelation therapy. Data. Field investigation: blood-lead in 81 people (50 children) plus soil and environmental sampling.
  • Lead Exposures from Car Batteries — A Global Problem · PMC / Environmental Health Perspectives Review documenting used lead-acid battery recycling as a worldwide exposure source. Summary. Documents used lead-acid battery recycling as a global exposure source: after the 18 child deaths in Dakar, investigators identified at least 22 similar contaminated sites, mostly in poor tropical cities where informal recyclers melt battery slag without protection. Data. Case review citing site investigations and blood-lead data (e.g. Haina, Dominican Republic, mean 71 µg/dL).
  • Blood Lead Levels in Children Living Near an Informal Lead Battery Recycling Workshop in Patna, Bihar · PMC / NCBI India case study of childhood lead exposure near an informal battery workshop. Abstract. Pilot study of 41 children (3–12 y) near an illegal lead-battery recycling workshop in Patna, Bihar: all had detectable lead, 91% above 5 µg/dL, mean 24.4 µg/dL (up to 80.1), five above the 45 µg/dL chelation threshold; haemoglobin fell as blood-lead rose. Data. Blood-lead and haemoglobin measurements in 41 exposed children.
  • Child Exposure to Lead near Informal Used Lead-Acid Battery Recycling Operations in Nairobi Slums, Kenya · PMC / NCBI Kenya case study modelling children's blood-lead near informal recycling. Abstract. Study of three Nairobi slums near informal used-lead-acid-battery recycling: all floor-dust samples and 71% of soils exceeded US EPA lead guidance (dust up to 58,194 µg/ft²). Modelling predicted about 99.9% of children under seven at risk of lead poisoning. Data. Soil and floor-dust lead sampling (Jan–Aug 2015) with blood-lead modelling.
  • Lead Poisoning and Car Batteries Initiative · Pure Earth NGO initiative targeting used lead-acid battery recycling health harms. Summary. Pure Earth's initiative targets informal used-lead-acid-battery recycling across seven countries through community health protection, soil remediation, policy (Basel guidelines) and economic alternatives; documents child blood-lead above 100 µg/dL beside informal smelters. Data. NGO programme description citing blood-lead thresholds and field observations.
  • Pervasive health threats of unregulated battery recycling · Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability Stanford summary of research on unregulated battery-recycling health harms. Summary. Stanford Doerr School feature on the pervasive health threats of unregulated battery recycling. (Page returned HTTP 403 — text not retrievable; noted from the title as context for the informal-recycling harm.) Data. News feature summarising academic research — no primary dataset retrieved.
  • Lead-Acid Battery Recycling · WorstPolluted.org (Pure Earth / Blacksmith Institute) Pollution case-study report on used lead-acid battery recycling. Summary. WorstPolluted.org case-study report on lead-acid battery recycling as a leading pollution source. (Site returned an SSL certificate error — page not retrievable; listed as a case-study lead; a companion report sits at display/65.) Data. Pollution site inventory / case studies — not retrieved.

Across the chain — market data & industry statistics 7

Where the chain's supply, use and price figures come from — mostly the ILZSG family, much of it subscription-gated.
  • ILZSG: Global Refined Zinc and Lead Markets to Face Significant Supply Surplus in 2026 · Shanghai Metal Market Reports ILZSG's forecast of a refined lead and zinc supply surplus in 2026. Summary. Reports ILZSG forecasts for 2026: refined-lead demand ~13.37 Mt and production ~13.47 Mt — a ~102,000 t surplus (91,000 t in 2025); lead mine supply up 2.2% to 4.67 Mt. A well-supplied, surplus market, not a scarce one. Data. Trade-press report of ILZSG demand, production and mine-supply forecasts.
  • ILZSG Press Release, June 2025 · ILZSG ILZSG's mid-2025 lead and zinc market-balance release. Summary. ILZSG's June 2025 press release — its half-yearly refined lead and zinc market-balance and forecast update. (PDF served as compressed binary — figures not text-extractable here; the balance/forecast numbers are reported in the SMM item above.) Data. ILZSG member returns on lead / zinc production, usage and stocks.
  • The Lead Market and lead price drivers · SFA (Oxford) Minor-metals analysts on lead demand, supply and price drivers. Summary. SFA (Oxford) lead-market overview: lead-acid batteries are >85% of demand; secondary lead exceeds 60% of refined production (recycling uses up to 65% less energy); China is around half of mine output — an unusually circular metal. Data. Specialist minor-metals market analysis — no primary dataset.
  • ILZSG Statistical Database · ILZSG Subscription database of monthly lead/zinc production, use, trade and stocks since 1960. Summary. ILZSG's interactive statistical database: country-by-country lead and zinc mine output, metal output and usage (primary and secondary split), trade with partner countries, and stocks and prices back to 1960; monthly and annual, Excel/PDF export, by subscription. Data. ILZSG member-government and industry returns, 1960–present (subscription).
  • The Study Group · ILZSG About the intergovernmental body behind the lead and zinc statistics. Summary. Describes the ILZSG: UN-established in 1959 for intergovernmental consultation on lead and zinc trade and market transparency; works through Standing, Statistical & Forecasting, Mine & Smelter, and Economic & Environment committees; a model for later nickel and copper study groups. Data. Organisational description — no dataset.
  • The World Zinc Factbook 2024 · ILZSG ILZSG factbook format; the lead factbook is published analogously. Summary. ILZSG's 72-page World Zinc Factbook 2024, a public companion to the World Lead Factbook, laying out supply, use, trade and recycling of the metal in accessible tables. (PDF served as compressed binary — tables not text-extractable here; cited as the factbook format.) Data. ILZSG compiled supply / use / trade / recycling statistics, presented as a factbook.
  • International Lead and Zinc Study Group (background) · Wikipedia Background / history reference on the ILZSG. Summary. Wikipedia background on the ILZSG: established May 1959 under the UN, now 25 national members plus the EU (about 90% of world lead and zinc), publishing monthly statistics and market analysis; the US is a former member. Provenance / context only. Data. Encyclopaedia reference — no primary dataset.
