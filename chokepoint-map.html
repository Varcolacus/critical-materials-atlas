The Chokepoint Map

Thirty-six end-use chains, each traced to where its real bottleneck sits — and sorted by what kind of chokepoint it is. The point of the layer, in one table.

Across the layer, one finding holds: the chokepoint is rarely the mine — and when it is, geology is why. Most bottlenecks sit downstream, in a refinery, a fab, a smelter, an enrichment plant or a single fuel — things that were built, and can therefore be rebuilt elsewhere given time and capital. Only a handful are fixed to a deposit that cannot be moved. Sorting the 36 chains this way turns a pile of findings into a decision: which dependencies are policy problems, and which are facts of the map.

What the map shows

Of thirty-six chains, only three — phosphate, the platinum-group metals and niobium — have a chokepoint that is genuinely geological: a deposit that cannot be substituted or relocated. Everything else is a built constraint: a refinery (rare-earth separation, lithium and cobalt conversion), a factory (solar wafers, chip fabs, battery cells, graphite anode), an energy input (ammonia's gas, aluminium's power), a policy (Indonesia's nickel ban) or a service (uranium enrichment). A separate five are coupled — by-products like gallium, helium and neon that cannot be scaled on their own — or timed, like copper, where the limit is the decade it takes to open a mine. The practical reading: most of these dependencies are policy problems with engineering answers, not permanent features of the earth — which is exactly why the distinction is worth drawing.
