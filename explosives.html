Upstream · the market above every mine

Explosives are a coal business

Blasting sits further upstream than anything else this atlas tracks, and a bottleneck there would sit above every mined material at once. That makes it worth measuring rather than assuming. In the one country that publishes the split, mining does take about 79.2% of industrial explosives — but coal is 56% of it and metal mining, the part that moves copper and nickel and lithium, is 9.0%. And where explosives trade does track metal output, it moves with the mine, not ahead of it.

Why detonators and not ammonium nitrate. 8.5 million tonnes of ammonium nitrate trade each year against 0.44 million tonnes of prepared explosives, because ammonium nitrate is overwhelmingly a fertiliser. A study built on it would be measuring agriculture. Detonators are made under licence, are not a fertiliser, and are needed roughly one per blast hole.

The signal is +0.71 in the same year and collapses to +0.13 a year earlier. Explosives track the mining that is happening, not the mining that is about to happen. As an indicator that makes them a confirmation, not an early warning — useful for checking whether announced expansions are actually moving rock, and not useful for anticipating them.

One measured counter-signal, from the same USGS survey: detonators sold for mining and quarrying in the United States fell from 37.9 million to 28.9 million between 2015 and 2019, while detonators for oil and gas rose from 1.7 million to 4.8 million. Through a period when metal prices were not falling, the blasting series was going the other way.

What would change the answer

Three limits, each of which is a route to a better test rather than a caveat to wave at.

  • Trade is not consumption. The four largest mining economies make their own explosives and are excluded. A consumption series — production plus imports minus exports, per country — would put them back in.
  • The end-use split is American. Coal is 56% of US explosives because the United States is a coal economy. In Chile the mix must be almost entirely metal, which is exactly why the Chilean correlation is the strongest here. No other country publishes the split.
  • A year is a blunt instrument. If explosives lead output by a quarter, annual data cannot see it. The monthly trade layer behind this atlas runs to 2026 and could.
