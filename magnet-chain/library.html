Research Library · Magnets

Rare-earth permanent-magnet (NdFeB) chain

A curated reference library of public sources for deeper study — gathered August 2026. Each entry links to a real source and carries an abstract (or factual summary) plus the data it rests on. A research-support layer of the Critical Materials Atlas.

A rare-earth magnet starts as ore that is barely concentrated in any one country, then passes through chemical separation, alloying and sintering — and at every step downstream China's share climbs rather than falls: ~60% of the mine, ~91% of separation, ~94% of the finished magnet. The mine is the least concentrated stage of all; the chokepoint is the separation plant.

Ordered to follow the chain: ① Mine (rare-earth ore, ~60% China) → ② Separation & refining (~91% — the real chokepoint) → ③ The heavies (Nd·Pr·Dy·Tb, the scarce magnet elements) → ④ The lever (export controls) — then the cross-cutting frameworks and trade data that sit across every stage.

① Mine — rare-earth ore 4

The least concentrated step: ore carrying many rare earths is mined in China (~60%) plus the US, Myanmar and Australia. Being in the ground is not the same as being separated.
  • BGS World Mineral Statistics — rare-earth mine production · British Geological Survey Long-run annual mine-production series by country, including rare earths. Summary. The BGS (and predecessor bodies) has compiled mineral production and trade statistics since 1913 — one of the world's largest such databases. Annual production by mass for 70+ commodities, including rare earths, is recorded per country and grouped by continent, with import/export figures to 2002; data is downloadable in Excel/JSON from 1970 via a web tool and API. Data. BGS annual national production returns compiled from official statistical and industry sources, 1913–present.
  • USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 — Rare Earths · USGS (2026) Annual rare-earth production/reserves/trade data by country, with US import reliance. Abstract. USGS rare-earths commodity summary: China dominates world mine production and holds ~36% of reserves; the US, Australia and Myanmar are the other main producers. US net import reliance is ~80%, with China ~70% of US rare-earth imports (2020–23) and over 85% of world processing sited outside the Americas; magnets are the leading use. (2026 PDF served 403 this pass; figures read from the USGS MCS rare-earths release via the USGS data catalog.) Data. USGS annual producer canvass plus reported import/export trade and net-import-reliance data.
  • China's Rare-Earth Industry (Open-File Report 2011-1042) · USGS (2011) Foundational account of how China built its rare-earth industrial base. Scope. USGS Open-File Report 2011-1042 documenting how China came to dominate rare earths — mining, chemical separation and downstream processing — and the policy, price and export history behind that dominance. (Landing page served 403 this pass; scope taken from the report's title and USGS catalog entry.) Data. USGS synthesis of Chinese rare-earth production, policy and trade — a descriptive report, no new primary dataset.
  • The Principal Rare Earth Elements Deposits of the United States (SIR 2010-5220) · USGS (2010) Deposit-level summary of the (thin) US rare-earth resource base. Scope. USGS Scientific Investigations Report 2010-5220 — the geology, distribution and resource potential of the principal rare-earth-element deposits of the United States, the domestic resource base behind any "reshore the mine" argument. (Landing page served 403 this pass; scope taken from the report's title and USGS catalog entry.) Data. USGS geological assessment of US REE deposits (grades, tonnages, deposit types).

② Separation & refining — the chokepoint 1

Rare earths are not rare in the ground; they are rare as separated, high-purity oxides. The solvent-extraction industrial base is ~91% China — and a new mine elsewhere does not replace it.
  • IEA — Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 (executive summary) · IEA (2025) Stage-by-stage supply concentration; China's dominance in refining/separation vs mining. Summary. IEA outlook: China is the dominant refiner for 19 of the 20 minerals analysed, averaging ~70% market share; its rare-earth stronghold lies in refining and separation rather than mining. Resources exist in Australia, Brazil and Vietnam, while separation and permanent-magnet manufacturing capacity is being developed in Europe, Japan, Korea and the United States. Data. IEA analysis based on S&P Global data, USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries and the EU Raw Materials Information System (accessed April 2025).

③ The heavies — Nd·Pr·Dy·Tb 3

Nd and Pr give the magnet its strength; the heavies Dy and Tb let it keep that strength hot — and they are geologically scarcer and even more China-concentrated in separation.
  • USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 — Rare Earths (Heavy) · USGS (2026) Separate heavy-rare-earth (Dy, Tb, …) commodity sheet; notes the Oct–Nov 2025 export-control episode. Scope. USGS commodity sheet for the heavy rare earths — dysprosium, terbium and others — whose separation is even more concentrated in China than the lights, and which carry the highest magnet-supply risk; it notes the October–November 2025 export-control episode. (2026 PDF served 403 this pass; scope taken from the sheet's title.) Data. USGS annual producer canvass plus reported trade and price data for heavy rare earths.
  • US DOE — Critical Materials Assessment (2023) · U.S. Department of Energy (2023) Federal criticality assessment covering rare-earth magnet materials for the energy transition. Abstract. DOE assessment of materials critical to clean-energy technologies. Neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium — the magnet rare earths for EV motors and wind generators — are assessed for criticality: dysprosium, neodymium and terbium rank critical in the short term (2020–25), praseodymium near-critical and more substitutable in magnets. Covers magnets, batteries, electrolysers and power electronics. Data. DOE criticality methodology (importance to energy × supply risk) applied to energy-relevant materials.
  • Rare Earth Elements: End Use and Recyclability (SIR 2011-5094) · USGS (2011) Where separated rare-earth oxides go, including magnets, and how recyclable they are. Scope. USGS Scientific Investigations Report 2011-5094 mapping the end uses of rare-earth elements — the flow of separated oxides into permanent magnets, catalysts, phosphors and alloys — and their recyclability, the context for the magnet end-use share and the recycling hedge. (Landing page served 403 this pass; scope taken from the report's title and USGS catalog entry.) Data. USGS synthesis of REE consumption-by-end-use and recyclability — no new primary dataset.

④ The lever — export controls 1

A downstream chokepoint turned into a policy instrument — the same playbook the atlas documents for gallium, germanium and antimony.
  • China Halts Critical Rare Earth Exports as Trade War Intensifies · The New York Times (2025) Reporting on the April 2025 export-licensing action referenced in the chain's chokepoint record. Summary. NYT (Keith Bradsher, 13 Apr 2025): reports China placing several medium and heavy rare earths and their magnets under export licensing amid the trade war, disrupting shipments to Western automakers and defence suppliers and underscoring the leverage of its separation and magnet-making dominance. (Article host not retrievable via this tool; summary from the cited reporting and headline.) Data. News reporting — interviews and official announcements, no primary dataset.

Across the chain — trade & commodity data 2

The datasets the chain's own figures are built from.
  • CEPII BACI V202601, based on UN Comtrade · CEPII Harmonized bilateral trade database (HS6, 1995–2024) used for the chain's magnet-trade table. Summary. CEPII database: BACI provides reconciled bilateral trade flows for ~200 countries across ~5,000 HS6 products, 1995–2024, derived from UN Comtrade by removing CIF costs and reliability-weighting reporter discrepancies. The atlas reads permanent magnets (HS 850511) from it. Data. UN Comtrade import/export declarations, HS6 bilateral, mirror-reconciled (CIF/FOB, reporter-reliability weighting).
  • USGS — Rare Earths Statistics and Information · USGS National Minerals Information Center Portal to all rare-earth data releases: supply, demand and flow of the 17 elements. Summary. USGS NMIC portal for rare earths — statistics on the worldwide supply of, demand for and flow of the 17 elements (scandium, yttrium and the lanthanides): the annual Mineral Commodity Summaries (1996–2026), Minerals Yearbook tables, and special publications on deposits, pricing, recovery and end use. Data. USGS annual mineral-commodity statistics (production, reserves, price, end use) — the production-data engine behind the chain's figures.

Gaps & good next searches

  • No clean HS6 code isolates separated rare-earth oxides, so BACI can verify only the magnet end (HS 850511) — the ~91% separation chokepoint is invisible in trade, and no public country-level series maps separation-plant capacity. That absence is itself the finding. Next: national environmental-permit and refinery-capacity trackers, or Adamas/industry capacity tallies.
  • Magnet-specific criticality literature (as distinct from rare earths generally) was not captured this pass — re-run "NdFeB substitution grain-boundary diffusion review" and "rare-earth magnet supply-chain criticality Resources Policy dysprosium terbium" for peer-reviewed anchors.
  • CSIS and Adamas Intelligence — strong known sources on exactly this chain — could not be reached this pass; prioritise them (and a direct search rather than relying on secondary citations).
  • No dedicated rare-earth / magnet trade-association source (a REIA-equivalent, or a magnet-maker group) was confirmed live — worth a direct search.
  • The 2005→2024 magnet-share history (~50%→94%) rests on two IEA anchor points, not an annual series; IEA's underlying data tables (or a JRC/Adamas series) would let the history chart be a real trend rather than two dots.
